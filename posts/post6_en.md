title: Reprieve

description: A wisdom tooth, an exam, and a sentence postponed again and again.

It went quiet again.

The morning before, the gum in my upper left jaw was still swollen. When I brushed my teeth, the taste of blood burst through the toothpaste in my mouth, reminding me that the wisdom tooth was still there. I pressed it with the tip of my tongue. It sat in a proper position, its edge smooth and even, like a criminal released on bail and awaiting trial. By the next day, the pain had withdrawn, and I had lost my reason to deal with it.

This is how I am always granted a reprieve.

That night, I came across a set of numbers from long ago. They were arranged neatly, like a case file closed years before. Yet one place in the sequence was visibly lower than the rest, sunken into the paper.

That dent belonged to Chinese.

At 7:08, my Casio watch rang again. These were probably the last few days I would still be pressing it silent.

I don't think I had slept the night before. My lucid brain and my eyes blurred by dried sleep proved as much. My heart, meanwhile, seemed to have grown into the bed board; no matter how I shifted, the board kept trembling under its command. Blood was being pumped in great quantities into the gum, and then came the waves of swelling pain.

There was no one left in the dorm. They must have got up long ago, washed, gone out, and joined the millions of examinees across the country walking toward the morning that fate had already arranged for them.

The room was too quiet for the gaokao—or rather, exactly as quiet as the gaokao should be. Only the dust lifted by sunlight along the doorframe, and the sound of doors opening and closing in other dorms, kept informing me that the gaokao was today.

I knelt beside the bed.

Extreme anxiety and pain left me no room to think. In truth, all I wanted was for this damned pain to stop, even if only for one day.

Not today.

I did not pray to myself for a good score, nor did I make a wish to the future. I was only begging a tooth, asking it to spare me for a while. At least not before the Chinese exam. At least not before I sat in the examination room, received the paper, and began reading those dense, tightly packed words.

No one answered.

When the final late-departure bell rang, my trial had arrived.

I washed in a hurry and left. I had not spat all the foam out; the mint of the toothpaste and the blood seeping from my gum mixed in my mouth, while from deep in my stomach rose the familiar burning sensation that followed every night of stolen sleep. I have never had a hangover, but I imagine a hangover is no more than this.

The corridor was already empty. The dorm supervisors had loosened the late-departure rules that day, though everyone understood that those conduct points had already become meaningless before the gaokao.

I walked out of the taste of blood and into a field of red.

The red carpet on the ground, the teachers in red clothes on both sides, the carp leaping over the dragon gate at the end of the passage. In all that scarlet, the wisdom tooth pulsed again and again. Only then did I remember that the bottle of mouthwash I had forgotten to use the night before was still locked inside my dorm cabinet.

Later, I could hardly remember how I entered the exam room. Only scattered fragments remained: the noisy crowd, hands stretched out for high fives, buzzing blessings in my ears, and my tongue constantly licking at the gum.

The desk in the exam room was colder than I had imagined. The transparent pencil pouch pressed against my wrist. The sound of test papers being unsealed came from the front. The red and the noise receded together, leaving only the opened paper on the desk and a deathly silence.

The wisdom tooth surfaced again. Unfortunately, I had nothing left with which to silence it.

A watch can be silenced. An alarm can be silenced. A late-departure bell stops by itself once it has finished ringing. But that tooth would not. Hidden in the deepest part of my mouth, it pushed against the gum with every heartbeat.

I pressed it with the tip of my tongue, trying to push the pain back. Of course the gesture was useless, but in that moment it was the only thing I could do. The invigilator's voice came from the podium, each sentence clear and loud, and yet I could not understand it. I lowered my head and looked at the open paper. The characters in the first reading passage were arranged neatly, so neatly they seemed almost indifferent. I knew every single character, yet I could not read a single sentence.

The words were, of course, Chinese.

I read them once, then again, and still had no idea what they were saying. Each time I almost grasped the meaning of a sentence, the upper left corner of my mouth throbbed, scattering the tiny bit of attention I had just gathered.

Later, I forgot the entire passage. I only remember that the author seemed to be Mao Zedong. The title, paragraphs, questions, and options all disappeared. What remained were the remembered pulses of pain and the panic of flipping the paper back and forth again and again.

Time had broken too. The clock above the blackboard always moved faster than the watch on my wrist. I tried not to let the sweat in my palm smear the answer sheet, but the second hand had already jumped to the composition section. My tongue had been rubbed numb by the swollen gum, and the source of the pain had quietly slipped from the wisdom tooth to the tip of my tongue.

The answer sheet, dampened by sweat, was easier to write on than I had expected. Unfortunately, what limited my writing speed this time was my brain, not the tip of my pen.

The bell rang.

When the paper left the desk, it made a soft rustling sound, as if that exam were being peeled off my body.

I do not remember whether the pain stopped then. I only remember that after the answer sheet was collected, it no longer needed me to deal with it.

The mathematics exam that afternoon felt like another cycle of the morning, except this time the hands pointed to three o'clock.

Unreadable words became unfinishable numbers.

Functions, equations, and geometric figures unfolded across the page one after another. The wisdom tooth tactfully retreated into a deeper place. Pressure took over from pain, allowing me to catch brief breaths inside pages of calculation—and this, absurdly, was happening during my gaokao.

In the exams that followed, I never saw it again. At least, I thought I didn't.

The thing that had once been large enough to crush everything ended.

Then came the crowd, the school gate, the sleep that was finally repaid, and a summer stretched out without end. Admission letters, suitcases, the first time leaving home: the gaokao slowly changed from a point in time that had happened to a topic old friends occasionally brought up.

As for that inflamed wisdom tooth, it soon became a footnote in my memory, like the admission ticket, the transparent pencil pouch, and the Casio watch that rang every morning—proof that I had once taken the gaokao, nothing more.

The admission ticket was later lost somewhere. The transparent pencil pouch slowly oxidized and yellowed in some drawer. The Casio is still here, only it no longer wakes me at 7:08 every morning.

Only the wisdom tooth still swells gently on certain mornings after poor sleep, like a hand reaching out from the gaokao to remind me that something remains there.

On another inflamed morning, I saw for the first time in the mirror the shape I had felt over and over with the tip of my tongue.

Perhaps because it had not fully erupted, this wisdom tooth sat lower than the normal molars in front of it, forming an unbeautiful hollow in the depths of my mouth. But it grew so straight—so straight that it did not look like a mistake, so straight that I began to doubt whether it had ever truly been inflamed the way I remembered.

The still-swollen gum objected.

But it was too straight.

So straight that it was hard to persuade myself to have it pulled out of my body, and then endure several new days of swelling, just because of a few badly timed inflammations. The pain was real, and its straightness was real too. Both facts lived in my mouth, and I chose to believe the latter.

So straight that when I mentioned it to my mother, she said, if you can leave it alone, leave it alone.

"A wisdom tooth is a tooth of wisdom."

Of course, this was not a medical judgment. But at the time, what I needed was not a medical judgment. I needed a reason dignified enough to accept.

A reason sufficient for me to exit the hospital registration page.

I took out the bottle of mouthwash I had not used since the gaokao. It was even more bitter than I remembered, and the swollen gum, obedient under the wash, subsided.

Later I discovered that the inflammation never lasted more than a day or two. So I even became too lazy to use the mouthwash. It was piled into the corner of the sink with the razor, dental floss, and several tubes of toothpaste nearly squeezed dry.

I watched the color inside the bottle fade little by little, from blue to green, then slowly to clear.

I never used that bottle of mouthwash again. The wisdom tooth, too, could always quiet down after a day or two of inflammation, as if nothing had happened.

I do not know when wisdom-tooth inflammation became a routine procedure inside my mouth.

When it hurt, I would think of hospitals, tooth extraction, anti-inflammatory medicine, and the bottle of mouthwash I had forgotten.

When it didn't hurt, I could remember nothing at all.

Or rather, this kind of judgment does not happen only in my mouth.

Once it goes quiet, I begin to believe in its straightness again.

Years later, when I looked at that set of numbers again, the Chinese score was still lying there. It did not continue to hurt, nor did it defend itself. It merely sank quietly into that spreadsheet. Like the wisdom tooth, it grew straight, yet on a certain morning it left behind a gap difficult to explain.

After watching several videos of wisdom-tooth surgery, I silently exited the hospital's official account.

It seemed we had reached some kind of settlement. It did not keep hurting, and so I was given another reason.

I only hope it does not wake again on the day of my postgraduate entrance exam.

It did not answer.

It went quiet again.
